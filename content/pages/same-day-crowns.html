The workflow

How the CAD/CAM workflow works

CAD/CAM stands for computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing — the digital process that lets a crown be made in the office rather than sent to a lab.

dentistry

1. Scan and prep

We numb the tooth, shape it for the crown, and capture the prepared tooth and its neighbors with an intraoral scanner. Several studies show intraoral scans take significantly less chair time than conventional impressions.[3]

verified

2. Design and mill

Software shapes the crown on screen, matching your bite and the tooth's color. A milling unit carves it from a solid ceramic block — the chairside process typically completes within about an hour.[3]

check_circle

3. Finish and cement

We glaze and heat-treat the crown, verify the fit and bite, then bond it in place. No temporary, no second appointment, and you eat normally that day.

Materials

Zirconia vs lithium disilicate

Both are metal-free ceramics. The choice comes down to strength versus translucency, and where in the mouth the crown will live.

Material Character Looks like Best location Watch-outs
Zirconia The strongest, most fracture-resistant ceramic we mill Opaque to moderately translucent Molars, bridges, heavy biting forces Can wear opposing enamel if not polished correctly
Lithium disilicate High-strength with excellent light transmission Translucent; mimics natural enamel Front teeth and premolars Less ideal for long-span or extreme-load cases

Same-day vs two-visit

One visit or two? A side-by-side comparison

Traditional crowns are still appropriate in some cases, especially for complex, multi-unit, or highly specific shading work. Here is how the two paths differ.

Factor Same-day (chairside CAD/CAM) Traditional (lab-made)
Visits One appointment Two — prep, then delivery
Impression Digital intraoral scan Putty and tray
Temporary crown Not needed Worn for one to two weeks
Waiting time About an hour, in the chair Days to weeks between visits
Best for Most single crowns Complex multi-unit or artisanal shading

There are still cases where a lab-fabricated crown is the better route. Long-span bridges, multi-unit cases, and teeth that need very specific hand-layered shading may benefit from a technician's artistry, and some insurance plans process both approaches identically. The single-visit path is best suited to a single damaged tooth — a cracked molar, a heavily worn tooth, or one that has had a root canal — where the crown needs to restore strength, not blend across a whole smile. If you have a fractured tooth right now, book through emergency dentistry and we will stabilize it first, then determine whether a same-day crown is right for you.

For patients working in the Financial District, that single visit is the practical advantage: a full-tooth crown can be completed over a lunch break instead of across two appointments and a week in a temporary crown that might chip or pop off.

Evidence

What the evidence says about fit and longevity

Two questions matter most to patients: will it fit, and will it last? On fit, a systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2024 compared lithium disilicate single crowns made with the conventional heat-pressed method and the CAD/CAM method. It found no statistically significant difference between the two workflows, and concluded that both produce clinically acceptable results.[2]

On longevity, a long-term review of chairside CAD/CAM restorations reported survival probabilities of roughly 97% at five years and 90% at ten years, with fracture the primary mode of failure and long-term sensitivity not a reported problem.[1] For zirconia specifically, a summary of systematic reviews found 5-year survival estimates for tooth-supported single crowns ranging from 91.2% to 95.9%, with chipping the most common technical complication.[4] A separate systematic review has compared lithium disilicate and zirconia CAD/CAM crowns made from digital impressions directly.[5]

“The survival probability of CEREC-generated restorations was reported to be approximately 97 percent for five years and 90 percent for 10 years.”

— Dennis J. Fasbinder, DDS, JADA 2006
