Plan types

How a PPO works, and how an HMO is different

A dental HMO (also called a DHMO, or capitation plan) is the other common plan type, and it works on a different principle.

“A PPO plan is regular indemnity insurance combined with a network of dentists under contract to the insurance company to deliver specified services for set fees and according to the provisions of the contract.”

American Dental Association, Types of Dental Plans[1]

With a PPO you can see any licensed dentist, but you pay less in network, where the plan has negotiated a fee. There is usually no primary dentist to nominate and no referral needed to see a specialist.[2]

A DHMO reverses that. The plan prepays the dentist a set amount each month for every patient assigned to the office, which then provides certain services at no cost or a reduced copayment. Members must generally be treated at a contracted office to receive any benefit. DHMOs often have no annual maximum, but they lock you to the network and usually require a referral for a specialist.[1]

Coverage

What dental insurance typically covers

Nearly all dental benefit plans split treatment into tiers and pay a set share of each, up to an annual maximum.

Tier Typical coverage Examples
Preventive & diagnosticUsually 100%, deductible waivedCleanings, exams, x-rays, fluoride, sealants
Basic restorativeUsually about 80% after deductibleFillings, extractions, root canals, gum treatment
Major restorativeUsually about 50% after deductibleCrowns, bridges, dentures, implants
OrthodontiaUsually about 50%, with limitsBraces and clear aligners such as Invisalign
Annual maximumTypically $1,000–$2,500The plan stops paying once you reach it
DeductibleTypically $50–$100Waived for preventive services

The annual maximum matters most for big cases: 32.8% of in-network maximums sit between $1,000 and $1,500 and 48.2% between $1,500 and $2,500, yet an ADA Health Policy Institute analysis found only 3.4% of patients actually reach theirs.[3]

Tax-advantaged

Paying with an FSA or HSA

A flexible spending account (FSA) or health savings account (HSA) pays for dental care with pre-tax dollars, cutting the real cost by your marginal tax rate.

The IRS treats amounts paid to prevent or alleviate dental disease as medical expenses. Publication 502 names cleanings, sealants, fluoride, x-rays, fillings, extractions and dentures, and the same standard covers crowns, bridges, implants and clear aligners.[5] Purely cosmetic work is excluded: teeth whitening does not qualify, and a veneer only counts where it restores function.

For 2026 you can contribute up to $4,400 with individual coverage or $8,750 with family coverage, plus a $1,000 catch-up at 55 or older.[6] An HSA balance rolls over year to year, which makes it a good way to save toward a $3,800 implant before you need it.
