Sedation & Anxiety-Free Dentistry

Sedation dentistry uses carefully monitored medication to make dental treatment calm and comfortable, from a light nitrous oxide lift to full IV sedation. This page compares the three main options, explains who is a good candidate, describes how we keep you safe, and introduces the Stop Paddle protocol that hands control of every appointment back to you.

Key takeaways

  • Dental fear is the norm, not the exception: a census-matched survey of US adults found 72.6% reported some fear of the dentist, with 45.8% describing it as moderate and 26.8% as severe.[1]
  • Nitrous oxide is the lightest option and wears off within minutes; oral conscious sedation is a tablet taken before your visit; IV sedation is titrated through a line for deeper relaxation.

  • Every patient here gets a Stop Paddle — raise it at any moment and treatment stops immediately.
  • Sedation is an add-on to skilled care, not a substitute for it: pair it with preventive & hygiene care to reduce what needs treating at all.
Prevalence

How common is dental anxiety?

Dental anxiety is a spectrum, so the reported number depends on how you measure it. Population scales that screen for clinically significant anxiety find a lower share than a simple fear question does.

A 2025 survey of a demographically representative sample of 1,003 US adults, published in The Journal of the American Dental Association, found that 72.6% were afraid of going to the dentist, with 45.8% reporting moderate fear and 26.8% severe fear.[3] Measured instead with the Modified Dental Anxiety Scale, the estimate for moderate-to-high anxiety sits nearer 19% of dental patients.[4] Both figures describe the same people: a large group who would benefit from anxiety-reducing care.

“Dental fear remains widespread in the United States, suggesting little progress in prevalence reduction.”

— Authors, “A Census-Matched Survey of Dental Fear,” JADA 2025

The clinical risk is not the fear itself but the avoidance it drives. Skipping cleanings lets small problems grow into painful ones, which requires more invasive treatment and confirms the original fear. Breaking that cycle early is the whole point of an anxiety-free practice — and it is why our new patient information starts with a conversation, not a drill.

Compare

Nitrous oxide vs oral conscious sedation vs IV sedation

The three options differ in depth, speed, and how much you will remember. The right choice depends on your anxiety level, the length of treatment, and your health history.

Option How it is given Onset & recovery Awareness Drive yourself? Best for
Nitrous oxide Inhaled through a fitted nosepiece Minutes on; clears in minutes after 100% oxygen Relaxed but fully awake and responsive Usually yes, after a short recovery Short visits, mild nerves, strong gag reflex
Oral conscious sedation A sedative tablet taken before the appointment About 30–60 minutes on; effects last hours Drowsy but able to respond to you No — you need an escort Longer visits, moderate anxiety
IV sedation Medication through an IV line, adjusted in real time Near-immediate; you recover before going home Deeply relaxed; many patients recall little No — you need an escort High anxiety, surgical or multi-tooth care

Nitrous oxide and oxygen is the most widely used gaseous anesthetic in the world, and the ADA notes it has an inherent safety margin along with at least 12 built-in delivery-system safety features.[5] It also has the fastest onset of the inhalation agents and, because it is not broken down by the body, it leaves your system almost entirely by exhalation.[5]

Candidates & safety

Who is a candidate, and what keeps you safe

Most healthy adults and children are candidates for at least nitrous oxide. You may be a good fit if you feel anxious, have a strong gag reflex, are sensitive to dental sounds and smells, need several procedures in one visit, or have found local anesthesia alone insufficient. We confirm suitability with a health-history review and, where a condition warrants it, check with your physician first.[2]

Safety is not a feeling; it is a checklist. Per ADA guidelines, sedation of any depth requires a documented evaluation, informed consent, baseline vital signs, and appropriate monitoring. For deeper levels, at least one additional team member trained in Basic Life Support must be present, and positive-pressure oxygen must be immediately available, alongside a fail-safe system and scavenging whenever inhalation agents are used.[2]

Because sedation relaxes you, a few precautions are non-negotiable: arrange a ride home if you receive oral or IV sedation, follow the fasting instructions you are given, and tell us every medication and supplement you take. Our team will walk you through all of this — explore the clinicians who will care for you before your visit.

The Stop Paddle

The Stop Paddle: control in your hand

Before any appointment begins, we hand you a Stop Paddle — a soft, easy-to-grip paddle distinct from a raised hand. Lift it at any moment and everything halts: the drill stops, the suction pauses, and your clinician steps back to check in. Nothing resumes until you say so.

This is the clinical technique known as the stop signal, or “enhancing control.” It gives the patient an agreed way to pause treatment, which studies of dental anxiety management describe as a way to increase a patient’s sense of control and trust.[6] NHS England’s clinical standards for dental anxiety management call the technique simple yet highly effective, noting it can even be graded from a resting hand to a full stop as anxiety rises.[7] The critical part is what happens next: when you use it, we respond immediately, because ignoring the signal would break the trust it is meant to build.

For patients who prefer additional support, the Stop Paddle pairs naturally with emergency dentistry for pain relief and with our 100% Pain-Free Promise.
