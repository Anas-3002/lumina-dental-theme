Laser Teeth Whitening

Laser teeth whitening is a dentist-supervised, in-office treatment that uses a high-strength peroxide gel to lighten your natural teeth in a single visit. This page explains how peroxide works, how in-office whitening differs from custom take-home trays, why sensitivity happens, and how long the results last.

Key takeaways

  • Whitening gel contains peroxide, which breaks stains into smaller, less colored fragments so teeth look brighter.[1]
  • In-office whitening uses 25–40% hydrogen peroxide; custom take-home trays use about 10–16% carbamide peroxide.[2]
  • When both are done properly, in-office and take-home whitening achieve similar final color.[2]
  • Light or laser “activation” does not meaningfully improve the result or reduce sensitivity.[2]
  • Whitening does not work on crowns, veneers, or fillings, so plan it before those restorations.[1]
The chemistry

How peroxide whitens your teeth

Stains are molecules called chromogens, and peroxide works by chemically dismantling them rather than scrubbing them off.

science

Stains sit inside the tooth

Coffee, tea, red wine, and tobacco leave colored pigments called chromogens in the enamel and the softer dentin beneath it. Surface toothpaste cannot reach them.

water_drop

Peroxide splits the stain

Peroxide releases reactive oxygen that breaks the double bonds in those pigments, making them smaller and lighter. The tooth is not bleached white — the stain is broken down.[1]

lightbulb

The “laser” is the activator

A light or laser may speed gel breakdown, but trials show it does not change the final shade or the odds of sensitivity. The chemistry and contact time are what matter.[2]

Compare

In-office versus take-home whitening

Both routes use peroxide and both work. The difference is speed, strength, and how much you keep up at home.

Factor In-office (chairside) Custom take-home trays
Peroxide 25–40% hydrogen peroxide[2] 10–16% carbamide peroxide[2]
Time to result Visible after one visit Gradual, over one to two weeks
Final color Comparable in trials Comparable; equal shade change
Sensitivity pattern More intense but shorter-lived Milder but can recur daily
Best for A fast result before an event Slow, controllable lightening and easy top-ups

A 2024 review that compared the two directly concluded they are equally effective and safe, and that some patients keep their color slightly longer with take-home trays because top-ups are easy.[2] Many patients choose a combined plan: an in-office session for the immediate lift, then trays at home to maintain it. A randomized trial found combined in-office plus take-home whitening held its color longer than in-office whitening alone.[3]

Comfort & longevity

Sensitivity and how long whitening lasts

Transient sensitivity is the most common side effect of any peroxide whitening, and it is expected, not a sign anything went wrong.

When peroxide penetrates to the dentin it can briefly irritate the nerve. Reported rates vary widely — in reviews, roughly 37% to 90% of take-home users and about 17% to 100% of in-office patients note some sensitivity — but it is temporary, and reviews find no irreversible damage to enamel or pulp at recommended concentrations.[2] In a head-to-head trial, in-office whitening produced more intense sensitivity than take-home, though the overall risk was similar.[4] We reduce discomfort with desensitizing gels, adjusted contact time, and by checking for exposed roots or untreated decay first.

“Some people who use teeth whiteners may experience tooth sensitivity. That happens when the peroxide in the whitener gets through the enamel to the soft layer of dentin and irritates the nerve of your tooth.”

— American Dental Association, MouthHealthy

Expect color to remain stable for roughly one to two and a half years before a top-up, with relapse driven by diet and smoking.[2] In a two-year clinical study of at-home bleaching, 83% of patients still showed a clinically meaningful shade improvement two years after treatment.[5] In San Francisco, where coffee and red wine are daily habits, we usually suggest a short maintenance tray session every six to twelve months rather than a full repeat.

Good to know

What whitening cannot do, and what to plan first

Whitening changes the color of natural teeth only, so the order of treatment matters if you are also planning cosmetic work.

Whitening will not change

  • Crowns, porcelain veneers, or tooth-colored fillings.[1]
  • Deep gray discoloration from medication or trauma, which may not bleach at all.
  • Stains caused by a dead or injured nerve inside the tooth.

Plan it in this order

  • Treat decay and gum disease first — a healthy foundation protects your enamel.
  • Whiten before choosing the shade of any veneer, crown, or filling so the color matches.
  • Keep up preventive & hygiene care and limit staining drinks to hold the result.
