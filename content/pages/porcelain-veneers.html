Porcelain Veneers

Porcelain veneers are thin, custom-made ceramic shells bonded to the front of your teeth to change their color, shape, or spacing. This page explains what veneers can and cannot fix, how porcelain and composite differ, the two-visit process, how long veneers last, and what they cost.

Key takeaways

  • Veneers are best for the front of the tooth — they change color, shape, and minor spacing, but they do not move teeth or fix a bad bite.
  • When bonded to enamel, feldspathic porcelain veneers have a five-year survival of 95.7%, and the ten-year figure may approach 95%.[1]
  • Porcelain veneers are placed in two visits; a dental lab makes the veneers between them.
  • Placing a veneer removes some enamel, so the treatment is not reversible.[2]
  • A single porcelain veneer averages about $1,765 nationally, but San Francisco fees sit above that.[3]
What veneers do

What veneers can and cannot fix

A veneer covers only the front surface of a tooth, unlike a crown, which covers the whole tooth. That small footprint is its advantage — and its limit.

check_circle

Veneers treat

  • Stained or discolored teeth that whitening cannot shift.
  • Chipped, worn, or irregularly shaped teeth.
  • Small gaps and slight crowding of the front teeth.
  • Uneven tooth length, giving a more balanced smile line.
info

Veneers do not fix

  • Significant crowding or a poor bite — that is orthodontic work such as Invisalign clear aligners.
  • Decay or gum disease: these are treated first, because covering unhealthy teeth makes them worse.[2]
  • Heavy grinding or a deep overbite, where veneers may chip or loosen.
  • More than mild discoloration caused by medication or trauma, which may need a different approach.

Porcelain versus composite veneers

The two families of veneer behave very differently over time. Here is the honest comparison we walk through before treatment.

Feature Porcelain Composite resin
Made how Dental lab, custom ceramic Sculpted and set chairside, often one visit
Stain resistance High — non-porous surface Lower — porous, can discolor over years
Enamel removed More (about 0.3–0.5 mm) Less, sometimes none
Repair Re-bond or replace whole unit Easier to add or polish
National average per tooth About $1,765[3] Substantially lower

Porcelain wins on longevity and stain resistance, which is why it is our default for the visible smile. Composite is a reasonable, reversible choice for smaller changes or a lower budget. If your concern is color rather than shape, dentist-supervised laser teeth whitening is usually the simpler first step.

The process

The two-visit veneer process

Most porcelain veneers are done in two appointments, with a short gap between them for the lab to work.

edit

Visit 1 — design and preparation

We remove a small amount of enamel from the front and sides of each tooth, take a digital scan instead of a putty mold, choose the shade, and fit a temporary veneer so you leave smiling.[2]

star

Visit 2 — trial and bonding

Your finished veneers arrive from the lab. We check the fit, shape, and bite, make any adjustments, clean the teeth, and bond each veneer permanently with a light-cured resin. Your bite is verified before you leave.

It can take a few days to get used to new veneers. If the bite does not feel right, tell us — an early adjustment protects the veneers. See our patient transformations & reviews to see finished work on real patients.

Longevity & cost

How long veneers last, and what they cost

Well-placed porcelain veneers are a long-term investment, provided the enamel bond is sound and you protect them.

A systematic review and meta-analysis found that feldspathic porcelain veneers bonded to enamel had an estimated five-year survival of 95.7%, with the ten-year estimate approaching 95.6%.[1] A companion review of other porcelain types reported five-year survival above 90%.[4] Veneers can chip or debond, so we advise a night guard if you grind and a mouthguard for sport.

On cost, a national procedural survey puts a single porcelain veneer at an average of $1,765, ranging from about $500 to $2,895, and a set of six to eight at roughly $15,486.[3] Veneers are cosmetic, so insurance rarely covers them. Because San Francisco lab and overhead costs run high, treat any national average as a floor, not a quote.

Veneers do not ruin teeth, but the process is not reversible, because a thin layer of enamel is removed to bond them. The tooth underneath stays healthy when it is cared for. That is why we discuss the decision carefully and confirm you want a permanent change before we prepare anything.
