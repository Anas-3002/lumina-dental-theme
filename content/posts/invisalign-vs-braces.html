Key takeaways

  • For mild to moderate crowding and spacing, clear aligners and fixed braces produce broadly comparable final results on objective grading systems.[1]
  • Aligners frequently finish faster — one meta-analysis found treatment about six months shorter on average.[1]
  • Braces still hold an edge for complex movements: torque control, occlusal contacts, extraction cases and rotating pointed teeth.[3]
  • Aligners only work if worn 20–22 hours a day; objectively measured real-world wear averages closer to 15 hours.[5]
  • Hygiene is easier with aligners because they come out, while reported pain and quality-of-life differences between the two are small.[4]

If you are weighing Invisalign clear aligners against traditional braces, the useful question is not "which is better" but "which is better for this specific bite." The clinical literature is now large enough to answer that with real numbers rather than marketing. Below is what the systematic reviews and randomized trials actually show about outcomes, treatment time, hygiene, compliance, cost and who should choose which.

What the evidence says about outcomes

The most common measure researchers use is the American Board of Orthodontics Objective Grading System (OGS), which scores a finished result on alignment, marginal ridges, inclination, occlusal contacts, overjet and root position. A systematic review of eight studies in BMC Oral Health found no statistically significant difference between aligners and braces on overall OGS score, even though the individual appliances had different strengths.[1]

A more recent meta-analysis of seven randomized trials reached a similar place: clear aligners actually scored better on some domains like buccolingual inclination and marginal ridges, while quality-of-life and pain scores did not differ between the two groups.[4] The honest summary is that both systems move teeth effectively, and the finished result is usually close.

"Both clear aligners and braces were effective in treating malocclusion. Clear aligners had advantage in segmented movement of teeth and shortened treatment duration, but were not as effective as braces in producing adequate occlusal contacts, controlling teeth torque, and retention."

— Ke, Zhu & Zhu, BMC Oral Health, 2019[1]

Where each system wins

Invisalign is designed around removable trays that tip, level and rotate teeth in small increments. That makes it excellent for crowding, spacing and mild-to-moderate cases. A dedicated systematic review of the Invisalign system concluded it is a viable alternative to conventional braces for mild to moderate malocclusion in non-growing patients that does not require extraction, and that it predictably levels, tips and derotates teeth — except for the canine and premolar teeth.[3]

That same review identified where aligners struggle: arch expansion through bodily movement, closing extraction spaces, and correcting larger front-to-back or vertical discrepancies.[3] For those problems, fixed braces remain the more predictable tool.

Treatment time: faster, but not by as much as advertised

Aligners generally finish sooner, though the size of the effect depends on the case. The BMC Oral Health meta-analysis found aligner treatment significantly shorter, by about six months on average.[1] A separate systematic review and meta-analysis pooled study data and found aligners associated with shorter completion time (pooled odds ratio 0.61), with no significant difference in how well teeth actually moved.[2]

There is a caveat for complex cases. In extraction cases, the speed advantage can flip: aligners have been shown to take longer than braces when teeth must be retracted into a closed space.[3] For a simple crowding case, aligners win the calendar; for a difficult case, braces may.

Hygiene and gum health

Because aligners are removed for eating and brushing, you clean your teeth normally and there are no brackets or wires trapping plaque. The orthodontic literature lists "superior facilitation of dental hygiene" as a genuine advantage of clear aligner therapy.[2] That matters for anyone with a history of gum inflammation. Braces, by contrast, create more surfaces for plaque to collect, which is why orthodontic patients are monitored closely — see our preventive & hygiene care for how those visits work.

Compliance: the hidden variable

This is where aligners live or die. Invisalign trays must stay in 20–22 hours a day, coming out only to eat, drink anything hot and clean your teeth. When researchers measured wear objectively with in-tray microsensors, the average was about 15.2 hours a day, and only 25% of patients consistently hit 20 hours.[5] Patients routinely overestimate their own wear time.

The upside is that compliance is coachable. When one large aligner clinic added app-based reminders and feedback, the share of poorly compliant patients fell substantially, with full compliance defined as wearing trays 22 hours a day on at least 75% of aligners.[6] Braces remove this problem entirely by being fixed — but you cannot take them off, which is its own trade-off. If your lifestyle makes 20-plus hours of daily wear unrealistic, braces may deliver a better result simply by not depending on your discipline.

Cost and coverage

Aligner and fixed-brace treatment generally fall in the same broad cost band; the driver is case complexity and duration, not the appliance type. Both are usually treated as a separate orthodontic benefit on dental plans, often with a lifetime maximum rather than the annual maximum that applies to general dentistry. So who should choose which?

Rough decision rules from the evidence:

  • Choose clear aligners if you have mild to moderate crowding or spacing, no need for extractions, and you will genuinely wear the trays 20-plus hours a day. Esthetics, easy hygiene and often a shorter treatment time are the wins.[3]
  • Choose fixed braces if your case needs extraction-space closure, significant torque control, arch expansion or complex root positioning — or if you know consistent wear is not realistic.[1]
  • Choose a hybrid if a clinician recommends starting with aligners and refining with braces for fine-tuning, which is increasingly common for harder cases.
