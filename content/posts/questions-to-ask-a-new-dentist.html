Key takeaways

  • Treat your first visit as a two-way interview: you are choosing a long-term partner in your health, not just booking a cleaning.
  • Verify the licence first. Every dentist is licensed by a state dental board, and most boards publish a lookup that shows whether a licence is active.
  • Ask specifically about sedation credentials, imaging, how treatment plans are phased, and who explains the cost — these are where good and poor practices diverge.
  • Infection control is checkable: ask about heat sterilization, weekly spore testing and whether a chemical indicator is used in every instrument package.
  • You have a legal right to a copy of your dental records — the ADA recommends bringing your history with you.

Choosing a dentist is a decision most people make quickly and then live with for years. This guide gives you a short, practical interview you can run at a consultation — nine questions covering licensing, sedation credentials, imaging, treatment planning, pricing transparency and infection control — so you can judge a practice on substance rather than a nice waiting room.

Why the first appointment is a two-way interview

The American Dental Association frames finding a dentist as a four-step process: start with the basics like location, hours and insurance network; build a shortlist; meet the dentist and staff before committing; and choose someone who works with you as a partner.[1] The point of a "meet-and-greet" is not small talk — it is your chance to ask the questions that reviews and websites cannot answer.

Here are the nine that matter most.

1. Are you licensed, and how do I verify it?

Every U.S. dentist must hold an active state licence. Dentists are licensed and disciplined by state dental boards, which typically offer an online lookup that confirms a licence is current and, in many states, shows disciplinary history.[2] It takes two minutes and it is the one check no review site can do for you. A confident practice will not mind you asking.

2. What is your training and focus, and do you refer when needed?

Most people are best served by a general dentist who coordinates care and refers out when a case calls for a specialist. The ADA formally recognizes 12 dental specialties, including endodontists (root canal work), periodontists (gum disease), oral and maxillofacial surgeons, orthodontists and prosthodontists.[2] So the useful question is not "which specialist" but "does this dentist refer readily when a case exceeds their scope?" A willingness to say "that is not my area" is a strength.

3. Do you offer sedation, and are you credentialed for it?

If dental anxiety has kept you away, sedation is often the difference between getting care and continuing to avoid it. The ADA publishes specific questions patients should ask about in-office sedation, including the training and experience of whoever provides it, whether assisting staff have current emergency training, and whether the dentist holds all the permits or licences required by the state dental board for the planned level of sedation.[3] For anxious adults, ask directly whether the practice provides nitrous oxide or oral sedation, and who monitors you throughout. 4. What imaging do you use, and how is it used in planning?

Ask whether the practice uses digital radiography and intraoral cameras — both expose you to less radiation than older film and let you see what the dentist is looking at rather than taking it on trust.[1] The stronger question is how the imaging feeds into the plan: a good dentist shows you your own X-ray or scan and explains the finding before recommending treatment.

5. How will you build my treatment plan — and will you phase it?

A combined plan that prices all recommended work is normal, because insurance estimates are calculated against the full treatment. What matters is whether the practice will itemize per procedure and phase the work. In almost all non-emergency situations you are entitled to treat one problem at a time, starting with priorities such as pain, infection or decay, rather than approving everything at once.[1] If a practice refuses to phase or refuses to itemize, that is a warning sign.

6. What will this cost, and what does my insurance cover?

Financial surprises are one of the most common reasons people distrust dental care. Ask whether the office is in your insurance network, how they handle pre-treatment estimates, and what financing exists for larger work. The ADA suggests asking whether staff are familiar with your benefit plan and whether they offer financial options for treatment costs.[1] A practice with published, transparent pricing and clear insurance and financing guidance removes most of that anxiety before it starts.

7. How do you handle emergencies outside office hours?

Dental pain does not keep business hours. The ADA recommends asking how the dentist and office staff handle emergencies outside of office hours.[1] A specific answer — a 24/7 phone line, a same-day emergency policy, or a clear referral pathway — tells you there is a safety net. "Call back Monday" tells you there is not.

8. What are your infection-control practices?

This is the question patients ask least and should ask most. Infection control in dentistry follows well-defined, checkable standards. Under CDC guidance, instruments that penetrate soft tissue or bone — including periodontal scalers and surgical instruments — are classified as critical and must always be heat-sterilized after each use; most semicritical items that touch mucous membranes should also be heat-sterilized.[4]

Two details separate a rigorous practice from a casual one. First, sterilizers must be monitored — a spore test should be used at least weekly, alongside chemical indicators in every package to confirm the sterilant actually reached the instruments.[5] Second, handpieces and reusable prophylaxis angles must be heat-sterilized between patients, not merely wiped down.[5] It is entirely reasonable to ask how these are monitored.

9. How do I get my records?

Your dental records belong to you. Under the HIPAA Privacy Rule, you have the right to ask to see and get a copy of your health records.[6] The ADA similarly suggests bringing your records to a new-patient consultation so the dentist can review your dental history.[1] A practice that makes record transfer easy is signalling that it expects you to stay on your own terms.

A note on second opinions

If something feels off, get a second opinion before treatment — not after. Once a tooth is treated, the question is closed. Choose an office with no business relationship to the first, and stop at two opinions: if two independent dentists disagree, pick the one whose reasoning you can follow when they show you your own images.[1]
