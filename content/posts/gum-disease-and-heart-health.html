Key takeaways

  • Periodontitis is common: 42.2% of U.S. adults aged 30 and older have some form, and 7.8% have severe disease.
  • Gum disease is linked to a higher risk of heart attack, stroke and other cardiovascular events, but that link is an association — not proven cause and effect.
  • The two leading proposed mechanisms are bacteria entering the bloodstream and chronic, body-wide inflammation.
  • A Cochrane review found the evidence that treating gum disease prevents cardiovascular events is very low certainty; the American Heart Association says there is no direct evidence yet that periodontal treatment prevents heart disease.
  • What is clear: healthy gums are worth protecting, and if you carry heart risk factors, regular dental screening is part of managing your overall health.

If you have gum disease, you have probably seen a headline claiming it can cause heart attacks. The honest answer is more careful than the headlines: gum disease is genuinely associated with cardiovascular disease, the biology behind that link is plausible and active, but the evidence does not yet prove that treating your gums prevents a heart event. This article separates what is established from what is still being studied, and explains what it means for your care.

How common is gum disease?

Periodontal disease — the more advanced form of gum disease, in which the tissues and bone supporting the teeth break down — is one of the most common chronic conditions in adults. In U.S. adults aged 30 and older, 42.2% have total periodontitis: 34.4% with mild or moderate disease and 7.8% with severe periodontitis, per National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data analyzed by the National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research.[1] The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reports the same figure — nearly half of adults 30 and older.[2]

Prevalence is not spread evenly. In the same national data, total periodontitis was highest among men (50.2%), current smokers (62.4%), adults living below 100% of the federal poverty level (60.4%), Mexican American adults (59.7%) and people who self-reported diabetes (59.9%).[1]

What periodontal disease actually is

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory infection of the tissues that hold teeth in place. It begins as gingivitis — inflammation of the gums caused by plaque buildup — and, if untreated, progresses as the gums pull away from the teeth and form pockets that trap bacteria. In the most advanced stage, the bone supporting the teeth is damaged and teeth can loosen and fall out.[3]

Why gum disease might affect the heart — the proposed mechanisms

The American Heart Association’s 2025 scientific statement, published in Circulation, summarizes two broad ways gum disease could plausibly influence cardiovascular health.[3]

Direct pathways

Bacteria from infected gum pockets can enter the bloodstream — a process called bacteremia — during chewing, brushing or dental treatment. This opens the possibility of direct vascular infection or bacterial involvement in artery walls.[3]

Indirect pathways

Chronic gum inflammation raises the body’s overall inflammatory burden. A long-standing body of work links higher systemic inflammation to the development and instability of atherosclerotic plaques. A joint consensus report from the European Federation of Periodontology and the World Heart Federation noted that several inflammatory and lipid markers are elevated in people with periodontitis, and found consistent evidence that periodontitis carries an increased risk of future atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.[4]

What the systematic reviews actually conclude

This is where careful reading matters, because the association is not the same as a cure.

The American Heart Association is direct about the limits of current knowledge. Its statement says that although periodontal disease clearly contributes to the chronic inflammation associated with cardiovascular disease, "a cause-and-effect relationship has not been confirmed," and that there is no direct evidence that periodontal treatment will help prevent cardiovascular disease.[3] What the evidence does support is the general principle that reducing lifetime exposure to inflammation appears beneficial.[3]

A Cochrane systematic review asked the harder question — can treating gum disease prevent heart attacks and strokes? It included only two randomized controlled trials, both judged at high risk of bias. For people with metabolic syndrome but no cardiovascular disease, the evidence was very low certainty and inconclusive. For people who already had cardiovascular disease, there was no reliable evidence at all. The reviewers concluded that further trials are needed before anyone can say periodontal treatment prevents cardiovascular events.[5]

At the same time, reviews of the intermediate markers tell a more encouraging story. A 2025 systematic review of 16 studies found that non-surgical periodontal therapy improved key cardiovascular risk biomarkers, particularly inflammation and blood-sugar control, supporting the integration of periodontal care into cardiovascular risk management.[6]

Question What the evidence says Source
Is gum disease linked to cardiovascular disease? Yes — a consistent association, including an independent one. American Heart Association, 2025
Is the link proven causal? No — cause and effect has not been confirmed. American Heart Association, 2025

"Your mouth and your heart are connected. Gum disease and poor oral hygiene can allow bacteria to enter the bloodstream, causing inflammation that may damage blood vessels and increase the risk of heart disease. Brushing, flossing and regular dental checkups aren’t just about a healthy smile — they’re an important part of protecting your heart."

— Andrew H. Tran, M.D., M.P.H., M.S., FAHA, chair of the American Heart Association scientific statement writing group[3]

One practice-level detail the studies mention

The same AHA statement reports that more frequent tooth brushing is associated with lower estimated 10-year cardiovascular risk — about 7.35% for people brushing three or more times a day versus 13.7% for once-a-day-or-less brushing, along with lower inflammatory markers.[3] This is an association, not proof that brushing more protects the heart directly — but it is a low-cost habit with no downside.

What this means for you as a patient

Ignore both extremes. Gum disease is not a proven cause of heart attacks, and it is not harmless. The sensible reading of the evidence is:

  • Treat gum disease for your mouth first. Stopping the infection protects your teeth, gums and bone. Any cardiovascular benefit is a plausible bonus, not a guarantee.
  • Tell us about your heart history. Blood pressure, diabetes, previous cardiac events and medications are all relevant to safe dental care. The consensus report found that delivering periodontal treatment is safe with regard to cardiovascular risk.[4]
  • Manage the shared risk factors. Smoking, diabetes, high blood pressure and excess weight raise the risk of both conditions. Addressing them helps both.
  • Keep routine screening. The AHA notes that people with one or more cardiovascular risk factors may particularly benefit from regular dental screenings and targeted periodontal care.[3]
