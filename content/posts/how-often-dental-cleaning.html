Key takeaways

  • The old universal six-month rule is not supported by strong evidence: guidelines now say the interval should be matched to your individual disease risk.[1]
  • High-certainty evidence found little to no difference between six-monthly and risk-based check-ups for decay, gum bleeding and quality of life over four years.[2]
  • Some adults do need three to four visits a year — those with active gum disease, diabetes, a smoking history or heavy plaque and cavity history.[5]
  • Skipping care is not free: 42.2% of US adults over 30 have some form of gum disease.[5]
  • The right interval is a shared decision — you should expect your clinician to explain why your recall is six months, twelve, or shorter.

"Twice a year" is the most repeated advice in dentistry — and the least evidence-based, as it turns out. This guide covers what guideline bodies and randomized trials actually say about how often you need a professional dental cleaning, who genuinely benefits from three or four visits a year, and what really happens when care gets postponed. The takeaway is not "go less often"; it is that the interval should fit your risk, not a calendar.

Where the six-month rule came from

The six-monthly check-up has been the default in many high-income countries for generations, but the practice was never built on strong trial evidence. As Cochrane's reviewers put it, six-month dental check-ups "have traditionally been advocated by general dental practitioners," and the evidence base for the interval itself is weak.[1] The rationale was biological, not statistical: plaque mineralizes into tartar (calculus) within roughly a day or two, and only a professional with instruments can remove it — so regular visits interrupt the process before gum inflammation and decay take hold.

What has changed is the recognition that "regular" does not mean the same thing for everyone. Guidelines now emphasize risk-based recall: low-risk patients can safely go longer, and higher-risk patients should be seen more often.[1]

What the evidence actually shows

The strongest single piece of evidence is a Cochrane review that pooled two randomized trials involving 1,736 regular attenders. One trial followed adults at 51 dental practices in the UK for four years, comparing six-monthly, 24-monthly and risk-based check-ups.[2]

The findings were counterintuitive. There was high-certainty evidence of little to no difference between risk-based and six-monthly check-ups in the number of tooth surfaces with decay, gum bleeding or oral-health-related quality of life, and probably little to no difference in moderate-to-extensive decay.[2] Comparing 24-month with six-month or risk-based intervals, the evidence was moderate to high certainty for little to no difference in the same outcomes.[2]

"Whether adults see their dentist for a check-up every six months or at personalised intervals based on their dentist's assessment of their risk of dental disease does not affect tooth decay, gum disease, or quality of life."

— Cochrane Oral Health, Recall intervals for oral health in primary care patients[1]

The INTERVAL trial, published in the British Dental Journal, reached the same conclusion across 2,372 participants: over four years it found no evidence of a difference in gingival bleeding or oral-health-related quality of life between six-month, 24-month and risk-based recall.[4] Notably, it also found that patients greatly value frequent check-ups — a reminder that the decision is not purely clinical.

Does the cleaning itself matter?

A companion Cochrane review looked specifically at routine scaling and polishing. For adults without severe periodontitis who already attend routine care, it found little or no difference between regular scale-and-polish and no scheduled treatment for gingivitis, probing depths and quality of life over two to three years (high-certainty evidence).[3] Six-monthly cleaning did reduce calculus a little more than twelve-monthly cleaning, but the reviewers noted the clinical importance of that small difference is unclear.[3]

Read carefully, this is not an argument against cleanings. It is an argument that for healthy, low-risk adults, the marginal benefit of an extra visit is small — while for people with disease or risk factors, the calculus changes entirely.

Who actually needs three to four visits a year?

Risk-based recall exists precisely because some mouths need closer watching. The National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research (NIDCR) data show who carries the highest burden of disease:

  • 42.2% of US adults aged 30 and over have some form of periodontitis, including 7.8% with severe disease.[5]
  • Prevalence is highest among current smokers (62.4%), people with diabetes (59.9%) and those who do not floss regularly (53.1%).[5]
  • It rises with time since the last dental visit — reaching 54.8% among adults with no visit in the past year.[5]
  • Nearly 90% of adults aged 20 to 64 have had tooth decay, and 1 in 5 have untreated decay.[6]

The exact number is a clinical judgement — the point is that a patient with active gum disease and a healthy patient with no history should not be on the same schedule. If a practice tells you "everyone comes every six months" without discussing your risk factors, that is a signal to ask more questions.

What happens if you skip cleanings

Two things drive the real risk of skipping: how much plaque and tartar you build up, and how quickly your gums respond to it. In a low-risk mouth, a longer gap often passes without measurable harm — that is what the trials above show.[2] In a higher-risk mouth, the same gap can let gingivitis progress toward periodontitis, the irreversible loss of the bone and tissue that hold teeth in place.

The NIDCR data make the stakes concrete: periodontitis prevalence climbs the longer someone goes without a dental visit, and it is the leading cause of tooth loss in adults.[5] Add that nearly 90% of adults already carry a history of decay, and it becomes clear why "wait until it hurts" is the expensive strategy — late treatment is almost always more complex and more costly than early detection.[6]

How to decide your own interval

Bring your risk profile to the conversation. A short, honest self-assessment:

  • Do you have gum disease now, or a history of it?
  • Do you smoke, or have diabetes or a dry-mouth condition?
  • How many cavities have you had in the last three years?
  • Do you floss or clean between your teeth daily?
  • Do you have implants, crowns or bridges that need monitoring?

Each "yes" pushes your recall shorter.
