Key takeaways

  • A same-day crown is a permanent crown designed, milled and fitted in a single visit using CAD/CAM technology — scan, design, mill, seat.
  • The workflow replaces the traditional impression and temporary crown with a digital scan and an in-office milling unit.
  • Chairside systems can mill ceramics and composite resins, including feldspathic and lithium disilicate blocks.
  • The evidence shows same-day CAD/CAM crowns are clinically comparable to conventional crowns, with no statistically significant difference in marginal fit for lithium disilicate.
  • Same-day treatment is not right for every case — long-span bridges, complex bite rebuilds and some implant or aesthetic cases still need a dental laboratory.

A same-day crown is a permanent dental crown that is designed, milled and fitted during a single appointment instead of being sent to a laboratory across two visits. This article explains the CAD/CAM workflow behind it, the materials a chairside mill can produce, what the clinical evidence shows, and the real cases where same-day treatment is the wrong choice.

What a dental crown is

A crown, sometimes called a cap, is a tooth-shaped restoration that fits over a prepared tooth or a dental implant. It covers the whole tooth to restore its shape, size and strength, and is used for teeth weakened by a large filling, damaged or broken teeth, teeth after root canal treatment, and teeth that are badly discolored or misshapen.[1] Because it covers the entire tooth, a crown differs from a porcelain veneer, which covers only the front surface.

How the CAD/CAM workflow works

CAD/CAM stands for computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing. In dentistry the term describes a three-part process: a scanner that converts the shape of your tooth into digital data, software that designs the restoration, and a milling unit that cuts the restoration from a solid block of material.[2] The first chairside CAD/CAM system, CEREC, was developed in the mid-1980s specifically to make same-day restorations in the dental office.[2]

In practice, a same-day crown appointment follows five steps:

  1. Prepare. The tooth is numbed and reduced so the crown fits snugly and in line with the neighbouring teeth.[3]
  2. Scan. An intraoral scanner captures a digital 3D model of the prepared tooth and the surrounding soft tissue, replacing the tray of impression material.[2]
  3. Design. The dentist uses software to shape the crown around the tooth, adjusting contours and the bite digitally.[2]
  4. Mill. A bloc of the chosen material is cut by the chairside milling unit to produce the crown.[2]
  5. Fit and bond. The crown is checked, adjusted and then cemented or bonded into place — with no temporary crown and no second visit.

Chairside milling and material choices

Not every material can be milled in the office, and the material you choose drives how the crown looks and how it wears. Chairside blocks are supplied as either monochromatic (one shade) or polychromatic (shaded) forms, and the available family includes esthetic and high-strength ceramics, composite resins and nano-ceramics.[2]

  • Feldspathic ceramic — natural-looking and gentle, ideal for lower-stress areas and aesthetic cases.
  • Leucite-reinforced glass-ceramic — a middle ground for single crowns, inlays and onlays.
  • Lithium disilicate glass-ceramic — a high-strength ceramic suitable for anterior or posterior crowns, implant crowns, inlays, onlays and veneers.[2]
  • Resin-based composite — more fatigue-resistant in laboratory studies and easier to adjust, though less stain-resistant over time.[2]

Zirconia deserves a note of its own. It is the strongest ceramic commonly used in CAD/CAM crowns, but its manufacturing workflow often involves longer sintering, so it is not always a true single-visit material. Your dentist will match the material to the tooth’s position and function.

How good are same-day crowns, really?

The evidence is more reassuring than sceptics suggest. A systematic review and meta-analysis compared the marginal fit of lithium disilicate single crowns made with the conventional heat-pressed technique versus the CAD/CAM workflow and found no statistically significant difference between the two methods (P = 0.49) — both produced a clinically acceptable result.[4]

Longevity data is also strong. A review of chairside CAD/CAM restorations reported survival of about 97% at five years and 90% at ten years for CEREC-generated restorations, with long-term survival rates similar to conventionally made restorations.[2] In a systematic review of digitally produced lithium disilicate and zirconia crowns, the most common technical complication was chipping — reported in about 5% of lithium disilicate and 1.4% of zirconia reconstructions over short-term follow-up — and no single material emerged as superior overall.[5]

When same-day crowns don’t work

Same-day milling is a tool, not a universal answer. It has real limits:

  • Complex or multi-unit work. Long-span bridges, full-mouth reconstruction and cases needing advanced layering are better handled in a laboratory.[2]
  • Some implant cases. Implant crowns often depend on custom abutments and components that are fabricated off-site.
  • High-aesthetic anterior cases. Where the exact shade, translucency and characterization of a single front tooth must match its neighbours, a technician’s hand-layering can still win.
  • Materials that need extended processing. Zirconia’s sintering cycle can mean it is not truly same-day.
  • Cases where the margin cannot be captured cleanly. Subgingival margins, moisture and difficult access can force a conventional impression or a two-stage approach.[2]

The chairside review also flags the practical barriers that have nothing to do with your tooth: the cost of the equipment, the training required, and concerns about colour matching in some restorations.[2] A practice that offers same-day crowns should be able to tell you honestly where its technology ends.

What to ask before you book

  • Will my crown actually be finished today, or is a temporary planned?
  • Which material are you recommending, and why is it right for this tooth?
  • How do you check the fit and the bite before you cement?
  • What is the warranty, and how do you handle a chip or a debond?
